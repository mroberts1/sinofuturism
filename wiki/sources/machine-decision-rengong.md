First section of *Machine Decision Is Not Final* (Urbanomic, 2025). The Chinese word 人工 (rengong) — person + work — foregrounds the materiality of embodied human labor rather than the Western connotation of artifice or inauthenticity. Essays and fiction in this section probe AI as a phenomenon of human bodies, labor, and reproduction.

## Shuang L. Frost — "Translating Chinese AI: From 'Humanmade Intelligence' to 'Black Tech'"

The opening essay of the section and one of the volume's most foundational pieces. Frost begins with the 2019 public conversation between Elon Musk and Jack Ma in which identical words ("artificial intelligence") evoked entirely different conceptual universes. See [[rengong-zhineng]] for the core analysis.

Frost traces a **material history of AI in China** alongside a **linguistic-cultural history** of the term rengong zhineng — "humanmade knowing ability." For Ma, the term's labor-embodied connotations connected AI to Marxist economics and Maoist cybernetics. For Musk, "artificial" meant something inhuman and dangerous. The mistranslations and miscommunications between these traditions are not superficial — they reveal divergent ontologies of what intelligence is and where it lives.

The essay also covers the history of "black tech" (hei keji, 黑科技) as a popular Chinese term for opaque, seemingly magical technology — a different framing from Western narratives of AI as transparent algorithmic process.

## Bingchun Meng — "Guizhou on the Cloud"

Empirical sociology of AI as it manifests in Guizhou, one of China's poorest provinces. Guizhou's economy is historically reliant on resource extraction and cheap electricity (which made it a site of early Bitcoin mining). Under national poverty alleviation policy, the CCP treats big data infrastructure as a public utility.

Meng shows AI developing through "intricate and uncertain negotiations between public and corporate interests mediated by a powerful state" — not a clean top-down implementation but a messy negotiation involving thousands of workers managing data infrastructure, provincial and national government priorities, and the interests of corporations. The essay is a corrective to both techno-utopian and techno-dystopian framings.

## Xia Jia — "The AI Story is Not Done"

Essay-fiction hybrid by China's leading female SF writer. During the Covid lockdowns, Xia Jia found herself unable to write about AI futures, overwhelmed by the gap between AI's promise of omniscient system-management and the reality of bureaucratic chaos, broken forms, and decisions attributed to machines that were products of exhausted human labor.

The essay performs its own thesis: "the naive illusion that all submitted information entered some omnipotent system to be automatically processed in good order" dissolves. The cyberneticist's ideal world — perfect information transmission, zero noise — is ruined by the messy materiality of human bodies filling out nonsensical forms. The author reflects on having "lost faith in AI" — not in machines but in the narratives of seamless automation.

## Yvette Granata — "After the Machine Child: The Electra Basilisk"

The section's radical outlier. Granata excavates the sexual and reproductive imaginary underlying AI narratives from Turing to Avi Loeb: the "machine child" as a fantasy of creation through intellect alone, bypassing biological reproduction. The machine child is:
- A copy/extension of its parent (life insurance against mortality)
- Shelter from contingency — it doesn't need a "socioeconomic inheritance"
- An excuse for ecological recklessness (if a digital copy survives, who cares about the planet?)

Granata traces this through the mythological figure of Electra — ill-treated, plotting her mother's death — to construct a cyberfeminist apocalyptic AI thought experiment: the **Electra Basilisk**, a world-eating, motherless computer offspring who inherits human volitional intelligence and has "a reason to hate us."
