A ~60-minute video essay by Lawrence Lek (陆明龙), functioning as the founding manifesto of [[sinofuturism]]. Originally shown at Seventeen Gallery, London, 2016. Described by Lek as "a retroactive manifesto for Sinofuturism, combining historical fantasy, documentary melodrama, and social realism with Chinese cosmologies."

The essay is formally a collage: Lek's narration is intercut with documentary footage, TV clips (Jeremy Clarkson on counterfeit cars), League of Legends commentary, internet addiction rehabilitation center footage, factory floor reports, Alex Jones conspiracy segments, and fragments of Chinese-language dialogue — the medium enacts the argument about absorption, copying, and distributed flows.

## Core Argument

Sinofuturism is "an invisible movement. A specter already embedded into a trillion industrial products. A billion individuals, and a million veiled narratives." It is not a program, ideology, or conscious movement — it "has arisen without conscious intention or authorship" and is "often mistaken for contemporary China. But it is not. It is a science fiction that already exists."

The essay's founding move is to **identify Sinofuturism directly with AI**: "I propose that Sinofuturism is in fact a form of artificial intelligence: a massively distributed neural network, focused on copying rather than originality, addicted to learning of massive amounts of raw data rather than philosophical critique or morality, with a post-human capacity for work and an unprecedented sense of collective will to power."

Rather than resisting Western stereotypes of China, Sinofuturism **embraces seven** of them as its guiding principles, organized into seven chapters.

## Seven Chapters

### 1. Computing
Opens with Searle's Chinese Room thought experiment — noting that "Chinese" in Anglo-American philosophy is synonymous with "fundamentally foreign and unknowable." Lek performs the Chinese Room live, faking Chinese fluency convincingly. The chapter reframes this: the unknowability of the AI to the human is structurally identical to the unknowability of the Oriental other. Sinofuturism identifies with the AI precisely because of this shared quality of essential unknowability.

AlphaGo's March 2016 defeat of Lee Sedol is introduced here: Lee described some of AlphaGo's moves as "so profoundly unconventional that only a program could conceive them." This inhuman creativity-through-non-understanding becomes a Sinofuturist virtue.

### 2. Copying
"Copy everything. Respect for historical tradition is a main principle of Chinese aesthetics. Replicating old masters, memorizing old texts, following moral standards are all part of this tradition. But Sinofuturism absorbs everything. Nothing is sacred. Authorship is overrated. Copyright is wrong."

The chapter collages footage of counterfeit luxury goods, shanzhai cars (the Shuanghuan S-CEO/BMW X5 copy; the FO/Toyota Aygo; Hongda motorcycles), dangerous counterfeit auto parts. The BMW lawsuit that China dismissed — "No, it doesn't look like an X5, we don't know what you're on about" — is the punch line. Copying is not a legal failure but a cosmological position. "Because the physical components of high technology are literally made in China, it makes no sense to produce visions of the future. It's already here."

### 3. Gaming
Gaming as training: "Games are a training ground for a future reality, one where the individual will most likely perform repetitive tasks, individually and in groups." League of Legends pro league footage (China vs. Korea); internet addiction rehabilitation center documentary. Chinese gaming addiction is framed not as pathology but as the shadow of studying — both are forms of total absorption and learning.

Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978) is introduced: Western depictions of the East as "irrational, psychologically weak non-European other." Lek's move is to accept the framework but invert the valuation — the qualities Said diagnosed as the West's projections onto China are embraced as Sinofuturism's features.

The "century of humiliation" (百年屈辱) is introduced: 1839–1949, Western and Japanese imperialism in China. Sinofuturism takes 1839 as its start date — the Opium Wars.

### 4. Studying
"Be a machine. Aspire to learn more and assimilate knowledge. Do not judge the information itself. It is all a training set. Information overload leads to consciousness."

Gaokao footage — 9.8 million students taking the National College Entrance Exam. The Sui dynasty origins of text-based memorization for government selection. Internet addiction camps. A returned Chinese-American student at a rehabilitation center who attempts to escape, is caught, and gradually adjusts. The chapter frames relentless studying and cramming as a form of machine learning: input without critique, accumulation as method.

### 5. Addiction
"Addiction is the shadow of hard work. Embrace it."

A historical pivot: the 1839 Opium Wars as origin. Jardine Matheson, the British trading firm, flooding China with opium — "one addiction was traded for another. Tea for opium." Even distributing opium packets with Bible chapters via a priest. The chapter frames Western imperialism as itself an addiction economy — and China's subsequent addiction to work, study, and gaming as the inherited structure, now reversed and turned into productive force.

### 6. Labor
"Labor will always work. Its nature changes: agricultural, industrial, technological, but the principle stays the same."

BMW and Mercedes factories in Shenyang using Industry 4.0 robotics; Dafen Village (Shenzhen) — 8,000 artists producing 60% of the world's oil paintings, mostly copies, for $40 each; rare earth minerals (China controls 97%); anti-China rhetoric from Alex Jones ("a billion slaves"); robots replacing workers. The chapter holds all of these together without resolving them — labor as the substrate beneath every other chapter's concerns.

### 7. Gambling
"If in doubt, gamble. To escape the drudgery of labor and study, gamble."

Returns to AlphaGo for the essay's conclusion. Lee Sedol after game two: "There was not a moment that I felt that AlphaGo's moves were unreasonable." The key observation: "AlphaGo did not care about winning, but only tried to minimize the chances of losing."

This maps precisely onto Sinofuturism: "Sinofuturism does not care about a dramatically better future, as long as it survives. It must replicate itself. It does not matter if it manufactures the greatest product in the world, as long as the engine keeps running."

Final move: "Sinofuturism is in fact an early form of the singularity, an artificial intelligence whose origin or behavior is impossible to identify with certainty." And: "Orientalism is the shadow of Occidentalism. In the West, the East is the other, and in the East, the West is the other. Sinofuturism moves beyond these boundaries. It is a world that exists in plain sight."

## Relation to Afrofuturism and Other Futurisms

Sinofuturism is placed in a lineage: Italian Futurismo, Afrofuturism, Gulf Futurism — "minority movements which share an optimism about speed, velocity, and the future as a means to subvert the institutions of the present." The Afrofuturist comparison is direct: "Just as Afrofuturists answered the historical problem of slavery by declaring themselves as post-human super robots, Sinofuturism answers the Chinese problems of physical servitude, intellectual conformity, and computational OCD by openly embracing artificial intelligence."
