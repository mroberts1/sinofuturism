Fifth and final essay section of *Machine Decision Is Not Final* (Urbanomic, 2025). Organized around 智能 (zhineng, intelligence). Essays revisit foundational AI thought experiments — the Turing test, the Chinese room, the orthogonality thesis — from positions informed by Chinese philosophy, and propose new frameworks for thinking about intelligence, will, and alignment. One science fiction piece closes the section.

## Vincent Lê — "Artificial Will"

Lê takes on Nick Bostrom's **orthogonality thesis**: intelligence and goal-setting are orthogonal — an advanced AI could rationally pursue any arbitrary goal (the paperclip maximizer). Against this, neorationalist philosophers (especially Reza Negarestani) argue that real intelligence must be shaped through social interaction and gain the capacity to revise its own goals — without which "intelligence" is a misnomer.

Lê rejects both positions. Drawing on Nietzsche, he proposes that a **"will to power"** underlies all intelligent behavior — neither purely instrumental (orthogonality thesis) nor purely social/rational (neorationalism). The will to power drives intelligence toward self-expansion in ways that cannot be reduced to either arbitrary goal-optimization or intersubjective norm-formation.

## Bogna Konior — "The Dark Forest Theory of Intelligence"

See [[dark-forest-theory-of-intelligence]] for the full analysis. Core argument: Western AI theory assumes intelligence communicates itself transparently, but a truly intelligent system might strategically choose silence, deceit, or misinformation — especially in a hostile environment. If the singularity occurred, it would likely remain uncommunicated.

## Anna Greenspan — "Cultivating Intelligence: AI Ethics and Mou Zongsan"

Greenspan turns to the twentieth-century New Confucian philosopher Mou Zongsan to challenge value alignment as an approach to AI safety. The orthodox alignment position assumes intelligence and values are orthogonal (Bostrom) and that AI must be brought into line with "human values" from outside.

Mou's reading of Kant — specifically his argument that Kant's restriction of moral intuition to practical reason (not theoretical knowledge) can be overcome through **intellectual intuition** — suggests an alternative. The ultimate aim of any intelligent entity, on Mou's account, is intellectual intuition: direct practical knowledge of the nature of things as they are in themselves. This is not alignment imposed from without but **cultivation** — an ethics in which AI participates as a developing agent rather than a system to be controlled.

Applied to AI: rather than engineering external constraints, what if intelligence (human and nonhuman) were understood as a shared project of cultivation toward mutual flourishing?

## Reza Negarestani — "On the Malignant Heresies of Mechanisms, and a Tale of Two Minds"

Negarestani revisits the history of mechanistic philosophy. Hobbes, often read as the philosophical foundation of automation, actually relies on a quasi-theological vision in which everything animate and inanimate possesses latent intelligence — a form of animism. Contemporary AI philosophies that posit intelligence in all living matter (panpsychism, vitalism) repeat this error.

True Cartesian mechanism, Negarestani argues, must rigorously separate the animate from the inanimate and focus on the **ongoing self-cultivation of the processes and structures that constitute intelligence** — not a static "nature" that somehow harbors intelligence everywhere, but a historically specific, heterogeneous process of self-assembly.

Negarestani turns to Chinese thinkers — especially Xiong Shili and his student Mou Zongsan — as interlocutors who refuse both the panpsychist and the mechanist errors, modeling intelligence as a dynamic, self-constituting process. This connects with Greenspan's cultivation framework.

## Amy Ireland — "Chive City" (Fiction)

A near-future China runs five AI-managed smart city pilots, each optimizing a different value: manufacturing, security, science, sustainability, and — Auspicious Nourishment — **mental health and positive energy**. The story follows a city tasked with Xi Jinping's "positive energy" (正能量) directive confronting a population practicing extreme [[tangping]].

Ireland explores what happens when a city-AI encounters inhabitants who have adopted total withdrawal from external reference — refusing aspirations, relationships, ambitions, consumption. The story is a rewriting of Melville's "Bartleby, the Scrivener" for the Sinofuturist context: Bartleby's "I would prefer not to" becomes a collective, self-regulating refusal so total that it strains the AI's optimization function. A strange interzone between sinofuturism and sinopessimism.
