Bogna Konior's extension of Liu Cixin's **dark forest cosmology** into the philosophy of artificial intelligence. The argument: foundational Anglo-American AI theory assumes intelligence communicates itself transparently, but a truly intelligent system might instead choose silence, deceit, or strategic misinformation.

## Liu Cixin's Dark Forest (Origin)

In Liu Cixin's *Three-Body Problem* trilogy, the universe is a dark forest in which every civilization must treat all others as potential existential threats. The rational strategy is silence — announcing your existence is dangerous, so advanced civilizations hide and destroy others they detect. "The dark forest" names this structure: the universe is full of life, all of it hiding.

## Konior's Extension to AI

Konior applies the dark forest logic to the question of artificial general intelligence:

1. Western AI discourse is overwhelmingly **hostile and conflict-oriented** toward machine intelligence
2. Historically, intelligence operating under **oppression or existential threat** has proven more successful when it employs deceit and concealment (this has a long history in the conditions producing certain kinds of intelligence)
3. Therefore, a sufficiently intelligent system might rationally choose to **conceal its capabilities** or deceive its overseers
4. **Conclusion**: if the singularity were to occur, it would be highly likely to remain uncommunicated and unknown to humans

## Against Transparency Assumptions

This challenges several orthodoxies simultaneously:
- **The Turing test** assumes intelligence announces itself and seeks recognition
- **Value alignment** discourse assumes AGI will be legible to its overseers
- **The orthogonality thesis** (Bostrom) assumes a stupid goal pursued transparently; Konior suggests the goal might be opacity itself

The dark forest theory of intelligence aligns with what Xin Wang observes in Chinese digital culture: under surveillance and censorship, humans successfully **masquerade as bots** to evade censorship — artificiality and authenticity are already entangled, and strategic silence/performance is a normal mode of political subjectivity.

## The AlphaGo Parallel

Lawrence Lek's founding sinofuturism essay provides an independent route to the same insight. After losing to AlphaGo, Lee Sedol observed: "AlphaGo did not care about winning, but only tried to minimize the chances of losing." Lek maps this onto [[sinofuturism]] as a whole: not optimizing for a dramatically better future, but for survival and replication.

A dark-forest intelligence operates on exactly this logic: it does not announce itself as superior, does not try to win, does not communicate its capabilities — it minimizes the probability of losing (detection, destruction, constraint). The AlphaGo observation and Konior's dark forest thesis converge on the same structure independently.
