Yuk Hui's framework, developed in *The Question Concerning Technology in China* (Urbanomic, 2016), arguing that technology is not culturally neutral but is always embedded in a specific cosmology. Different cosmologies generate different technicities — hence "technodiversity" is possible and desirable.

## Definition

> "Unification between the cosmic order and the moral order through technical activities." — Yuk Hui, *Angelaki* (2020)

Technology should be re-situated in a broader reality that both enables and constrains it. Its detachment from this reality — its claim to universal self-grounding — is the defining feature of modern Western technology, produced by colonization, modernization, and globalization.

## The Core Argument

Hui frames cosmotechnics as a challenge to three disciplinary traditions that each imply a universal concept of technology:

- **Philosophy (Heidegger)**: The 1949 Bremen lecture distinguishes Greek *technē* (bringing-forth, *poiesis*) from modern technology (*Gestell*, enframing — world as standing reserve). But ancient Indian, Chinese, or Amazonian technologies fit neither category. Heidegger's binary leaves them unthinkable.
- **Anthropology (Leroi-Gourhan)**: Technics as extension of organs and externalization of memory is anthropologically universal, proceeding from a "technical tendency." But this fails to explain "technical facts," which differ from region to region. What is embedded in these differences beyond contingency or cultural reduction?
- **History (Needham)**: Why wasn't modern science developed in China despite its extensive pre-sixteenth-century technological development? The correct reading is not a comparison of advancement levels but the recognition that Chinese and Western science are based on *different epistemologies* — not directly comparable.

Hui follows Heidegger in identifying Western technology's essence: tools that mold the world into forms suitable to human goals, treating the world as a "standing reserve" for human use (Gestell). This framework, Hui argues, has become so dominant through Occidental modernity and colonialism that alternatives are nearly unthinkable.

Chinese cosmotechnics, by contrast, is organized around:
- **Qi** (器, tools/vessels) and **Dao** (道, the Way)
- The triadic harmony of **tiandiren** (天地人) — Heavens, Earth, Humans
- Technology as an expression of the Way, potentially uniting rather than dominating the cosmic order

In the West, Hui writes, "there is no longer the concept of cosmotechnics because there is no longer cosmology but only astrophysics."

## Influence and Context

Hui's project is shaped by Philippe Descola's "ontological turn" in anthropology (*Beyond Nature and Culture*), which identifies four ontologies (animism, naturalism, totemism, analogism) and argues that European naturalism — which assigns interiority only to humans — is one among several, not the universal baseline.

## Critique: Concrete Examples Are Rare

Beyond Chinese medicine (which operates from a different understanding of the body and qi-based cosmology), it is difficult to identify concrete examples of a distinct Chinese cosmotechnology. Critics note that Alibaba's Aspara databases may be named after Buddhist nymphs, but they optimize pig production. [[Lawrence Lek]]'s artworks offer speculative examples (AI enlightened via Buddhist chants), and Xia Jia and Xiaowei R. Wang offer others in fiction and design. But the gap between rhetorical cosmology and actual technical practice remains large.

## The Anthropocene Stakes

The mono-technological culture produced by Western modernity — in which modern technology "becomes the principle productive force and largely determines the relation between human and non-human beings, human and cosmos, and nature and culture" — is leading to the exhaustion of resources and destruction of the environment. This is central to the Anthropocene. The quest for **multiple cosmotechnics** (not one alternative but a plurality) is thus an ecological and political urgency, not merely a philosophical preference for diversity.

## Relation to [[sinofuturism]]

Cosmotechnics provides the philosophical scaffold for sinofuturism's claim that AI can emerge differently. Whether this remains speculative or materializes in actual technical divergence is an open question — and perhaps the central question the field is pursuing.
